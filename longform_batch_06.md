# Long-Form Expansion Batch Six

## Chapter Fourteen — The Children’s Game

The girls made their own tarot deck out of index cards.

Skyler discovered it beneath the couch after returning from Lena’s apartment. The cards had been cut unevenly. Some still carried blue lines from the notebook paper they had been made from. The drawings were bright and blunt.

**THE TOWER:** A pile of dirty laundry.

**THE MOON:** A spoon reflecting the kitchen light.

**THE DEVIL:** A television with too many buttons.

**THE STAR:** A bowl of strawberries.

There were no swords. No blood. No falling bodies.

“What does this one mean?” Skyler asked.

Luna looked over the back of the couch. “Which one?”

He held up the card with a number written on it.

**444.**

“That means four people have to help clean the room.”

“Why four?”

“Because there are four of us.”

“There are four of you?”

“Sometimes you count as two.”

Summer laughed from the floor. Alexis was building a tower out of plastic cups.

Skyler sat among the cards. The house smelled of crayons and toast. A cartoon played on the television, but the sound was low enough that no one had to listen.

“Did you make all of these?” he asked.

“We found the pictures online,” Alexis said.

Skyler’s body tightened.

“Which pictures?”

“The ones with the doors.”

“Where?”

“On your tablet.”

He looked toward the kitchen.

Hanna came through the doorway carrying laundry. “I told them not to use your accounts.”

Skyler stood too quickly.

“Which account?”

Hanna set down the basket. “The old tablet. It was already open.”

He walked to the kitchen. The tablet displayed a blank screen. He checked the history. An image search. Four doors. Tarot cards. Angel numbers. Children’s games.

Nothing unusual.

That was the problem.

He returned to the living room.

“Did anyone tell you what the numbers mean?”

Luna shrugged. “People say different things.”

“Like what?”

“Some say they’re messages.”

“Do you think they are?”

She studied him.

“I think you want them to be.”

The sentence landed with a child’s accuracy.

Skyler looked at the cards spread across the carpet.

“What do you think they are?”

“Pictures people use when they don’t know what to say.”

He sat down again.

Hanna folded laundry. The domestic movement steadied the room.

“Your father keeps asking the world to tell him what happened,” she said. “You are allowed not to know everything.”

Skyler looked at her.

“I don’t know how to protect you if I don’t know.”

“You protect us by not making us carry your fear.”

Luna placed a card in his lap. The Star. Strawberries beneath a yellow sun.

“What does it mean?” Skyler asked.

“It means you have to eat one.”

He ate the strawberry.

It was too sour.

The girls screamed with laughter.

For ten minutes, the world became exactly what it was: a living room, a basket of clothes, a bad strawberry, and a father trying not to turn love into surveillance.

## Chapter Sixteen — The First Leak

The first leak came from a woman who had not meant to become a source.

Anika worked in an office where the lights turned off automatically if no one moved for eight minutes. She had learned to wave one hand every few minutes so the screens would not go dark while she read the files.

The file she copied was not dramatic. No list of leaders. No master key. Only a procurement request for narrative analysis software and a set of internal notes about “high-value ambiguity targets.”

Skyler’s name appeared on the third page.

Anika printed the document, folded it into quarters, and placed it inside a cookbook.

At home, her brother called.

“You sound tired,” he said.

“I am tired.”

“Did they do something?”

“No.”

“That’s not an answer.”

She looked at the cookbook on the table.

“I found a record.”

“Then take it to someone.”

“Who?”

“Someone who wants the truth.”

Anika laughed. “Everyone wants the truth. They want it to arrive shaped like their politics.”

She contacted Lena through an encrypted form she did not trust. Lena replied with a single question.

**What can the document prove?**

Anika almost deleted the exchange.

She had expected gratitude. She had expected urgency. She had not expected a question that returned responsibility to her.

The answer took two days.

**It proves that a company purchased narrative analysis related to Skyler’s profile. It does not prove the company directed the campaign. It does not prove the profile was accurate. It does not prove who authorized the purchase.**

Lena published a short note, not the document. The note described a procurement pattern without naming Anika.

For three hours, nothing happened.

Then an internal account posted a screenshot of Anika’s office schedule.

She received a message from an unknown number.

**YOU WANTED TO BE SEEN. NOW YOU ARE.**

Her brother called again.

This time she answered.

“Come home,” he said.

“I can’t.”

“Why?”

“Because I have to finish something.”

“You don’t have to save the world.”

“I know.”

“Then what are you doing?”

Anika looked at the screen.

“Trying not to become the kind of person who sees harm and calls silence neutral.”

The next leak she released included her own name.

She did not explain it. She did not ask anyone to forgive her. She attached a statement:

**I participated in the profiling system described here. I benefited from work that harmed people. The records below show what I knew at the time and what I did not know. I am releasing them so the distinction can be checked rather than trusted.**

Mara read the statement in the church library.

“She has made herself vulnerable,” Skyler said.

“She has made herself accountable,” Mara replied.

“Those are not the same.”

“No. Accountability is the choice to stop treating vulnerability as an excuse.”

Skyler thought of the phone, the screens, the numbers, the cards. Every system wanted a person to become simpler than they were.

Anika had made herself harder to simplify.

## Chapter Eighteen — The Second Offer

Thorne sent the second offer through a lawyer.

The lawyer’s office occupied a glass tower with a lobby full of plants no one touched. Skyler sat beneath a photograph of a mountain and waited while a receptionist watched him through a camera disguised as a smoke detector.

The lawyer introduced herself as Ms. Vale. She was not related to Imani. She said this before Skyler asked.

“I represent a private consortium,” she said.

“Which means?”

“Which means the client values discretion.”

“Everyone who says that values control.”

She opened a folder.

The offer was not twenty million this time. It was smaller and more dangerous: enough to clear the debt, restore a house, and fund a legal campaign regarding his family.

Skyler felt the number move through him.

“You can have your life back,” Ms. Vale said.

“No.”

“You can have a version of it.”

The language was honest enough to hurt.

He read the contract. His work would involve “narrative integrity,” “identity risk,” and “conflict mediation.” The terms were vague by design.

“What happens if I refuse?”

“Your refusal is yours.”

“Thorne said something similar.”

“Mr. Thorne is not my client.”

She slid a second document across the table.

A photograph of his former house. A record of a closed family case. A list of dates when he had missed visits with his daughters.

Some dates were correct. One was impossible. He had been in a hospital that day.

Skyler looked at the lawyer.

“Where did this come from?”

“I am not authorized to discuss sources.”

The impossible date became the most important thing in the room.

He took a photograph with his old camera.

Ms. Vale watched. “You are not signing?”

“No.”

“Then why come?”

“To understand the shape of the trap.”

She smiled. “You think refusing makes you free.”

“No. I think refusing gives me one decision they cannot make for me.”

Outside, the city glowed beneath the glass tower. Skyler carried the documents to Mara. She compared the dates with the hospital record.

The impossible date was not a mistake.

It belonged to another Skyler Spillers in a different state.

The Ledger had fused two identities.

A person had been turned into a system error, then the error had been treated as a moral fact.

Skyler sat at the church table and looked at the duplicate record.

He had spent months asking who was watching him.

Now he understood another danger: a machine did not need to hate you to destroy you. It only needed to confuse you with someone else and remain confident.

## Chapter Twenty-Seven — The Prevention Story

Anika met Ghost in the stairwell because she refused to enter the Mirror Room again.

“You lied to me,” she said.

Ghost did not ask how.

“You told me you only translated profiles. You chose targets.”

“Yes.”

“You told me the system predicted violence. It sometimes produced suspicion.”

“Yes.”

“You told me you were trying to stop the Society.”

“I am trying now.”

Anika stepped closer. “Now is not a moral category.”

The stairwell smelled of concrete and bleach. A fluorescent bulb blinked overhead.

Ghost held out the envelope containing his confession about the security guard.

“I made contact. It harmed him. I am preserving the record.”

“Preserving is not repairing.”

“No.”

“Then why keep it?”

“Because if I erase it, I can tell myself the harm happened without me.”

Anika looked at the envelope.

She had a similar one in her own bag.

Inside was the original profile she had edited before the leak. She had removed a paragraph about her brother because she feared the Society would discover the connection.

“Take this,” she said.

Ghost did not move.

“I altered the file,” she continued. “Not to protect someone else. To protect myself.”

He accepted the envelope.

They stood beneath the blinking light, two people handing one another evidence that made them less innocent and more real.

“Why did you leave?” Ghost asked.

“Because I believed prevention was a clean word.”

“And now?”

“Now I think prevention is often what people call control when they are afraid to admit they want power.”

Ghost looked toward the sealed door.

“Do you think we can make a system that does not become that?”

“No.”

The answer surprised him.

“We can make a system that notices when it is becoming that,” she said. “That is the best we get.”

The bulb steadied.

They went upstairs.
