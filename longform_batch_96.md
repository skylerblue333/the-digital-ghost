# Long-Form Expansion Batch Ninety-Six

## Chapter One Hundred Thirty-Two — The Last Request

The last request arrived from a person Skyler did not know.

They asked the library to remove a record that had been cited in three public reports. The record was accurate, but the person said its continued availability had made ordinary life impossible.

The archive had no simple answer.

Accuracy supported retention. Harm supported removal. The person had consented years earlier under different circumstances. The original agreement allowed withdrawal, but the process required a written explanation.

Mara wanted to waive the requirement.

A custodian warned that doing so would create an exception future applicants could not rely on.

Skyler was asked to advise.

He said, “The rule was written to protect people from arbitrary removal. It should not become a reason to make someone prove their suffering.”

The record was temporarily restricted while the panel reviewed the request. The reports were amended to remove identifying details. The archive notified every known recipient and asked them to stop redistributing the old version.

Not everyone complied.

Some argued that public information could not become private again.

The panel rejected the idea that publication erased all obligations. Public availability was a fact, not a moral permission.

After six weeks, the record was removed from open access and preserved under restricted custody. The person received a written explanation of what had changed, who could still access the record, and what would happen at the next review.

The decision did not erase the past.

It reduced the future harm.

Skyler read the final notice and recognized the Four Pillars in a different order. Consequence had forced a reconsideration of consent. Consent had changed what memory could remain public. Memory had altered the person’s identity in the eyes of institutions.

The pillars were not columns.

They were weather systems crossing one another.

The person sent a short reply:

**Thank you for not calling the record false just because it hurt me. Thank you for not calling the harm inevitable just because the record was true.**

Skyler saved the reply only with permission.

The archive added a new principle:

**Truth does not cancel the duty to reduce avoidable harm.**

The principle remained difficult to apply.

That difficulty was part of its protection.

A system that offered easy answers would eventually offer easy cruelty.
