# Long-Form Expansion Batch Fifty

## Chapter Eighty-Six — The Audit of Us

The public access audit began with thirty-two decisions.

Ten had been delayed. Seven had been redacted. Four had been denied. Three had been removed after review. The remaining eight had been approved without dispute.

The auditors found a pattern.

Requests written in formal language were answered faster. Requests from people who already knew the project’s vocabulary received more detailed explanations. Requests that mentioned the Society were treated as higher risk even when the supporting evidence was thin.

Open Door had reproduced the very hierarchy it had been built to challenge.

Skyler read the report twice.

The first time, he looked for errors.

The second time, he looked for excuses.

He found several.

At the public meeting, the auditors presented their findings. One volunteer argued that the delays were caused by limited staff. Another said the pattern did not prove discrimination.

The lead auditor agreed.

“It does not prove motive,” she said. “It does establish unequal outcomes.”

The room quieted.

Skyler stood.

“We will publish the full report.”

A volunteer asked whether the public would use it to attack them.

“Yes.”

“Then why release it?”

“Because we have asked other institutions to show how their systems failed. We cannot make an exception for ourselves.”

The report went online that evening.

A journalist called it a scandal. Another called it evidence that the project worked. Both descriptions were too simple.

Open Door created a correction plan: plain-language intake, multilingual access, an independent delay review, and a public dashboard showing the age of unresolved requests. The changes required money they did not have.

They reduced other programs.

The decision caused anger.

People wanted the project to be both accessible and expansive, fast and careful, open and private. No arrangement could satisfy every demand.

At the next council meeting, Skyler suggested using a predictive queue to identify urgent requests.

Noor rejected the proposal.

“You are trying to solve inequality with a ranking system,” she said.

He looked at her.

“Then what do you suggest?”

“Human triage with reasons that can be challenged.”

“It will be slower.”

“Yes.”

He nodded.

The audit had not made them perfect.

It had made their imperfections harder to hide.

That was a beginning with consequences.
