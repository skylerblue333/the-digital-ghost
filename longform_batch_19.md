# Long-Form Expansion Batch Nineteen

## Chapter Fifty-Four — The Reviewers

The independent reviewers arrived with no cameras and no slogans.

There were five of them: a public defender, a librarian, a software auditor, a tenant organizer, and a retired judge. None had worked for Aegis. None had signed the Society’s contracts. Their only authority came from the fact that the project had agreed to let them disagree publicly.

They spent three weeks reading the logs.

On the fourth day, the auditor found a gap.

“Your access records do not show who viewed the emergency files,” she said.

Imani checked the system. “Those records were excluded because emergency access requires a separate privacy layer.”

“Then the audit is incomplete.”

“Yes.”

“You knew?”

“We knew the layer existed. We did not know the gap could be used this way.”

The auditor closed her notebook.

“Then your public claim of complete transparency is false.”

Skyler felt the old urge to defend the project. He had spent months building it. He wanted criticism to recognize effort before describing failure.

The public defender spoke first.

“Failure is not fraud.”

The librarian added, “But hiding failure becomes a kind of fraud.”

They published a correction that afternoon.

**Open Door’s emergency access logs are incomplete. The project will not claim otherwise.**

The statement received less attention than the original report. That was acceptable. Accuracy did not require applause.

A week later, the Society fractured.

One faction released evidence that the arbitration council had purchased narrative campaigns without informing lower-level contractors. Another faction accused Thorne of protecting Skyler. A third faction claimed the entire conflict was an internal simulation.

The public tried to choose the true faction.

Skyler refused.

A system that had lied for years did not become honest simply because its members began telling different stories about one another.

The reviewers issued their report with three recommendations: preserve refusal logs, publish access limitations, and create a process for removing people who used the archive to target others.

The third recommendation frightened Skyler most.

“Who decides removal?” he asked.

The retired judge looked at him.

“People who can be removed too.”

The answer became the project’s final structural rule.

No one received permanent authority.

Not even the founders.

Especially not the founders.
