# Long-Form Expansion Batch Twenty

## Chapter Fifty-Five — The Guardian Problem

The board offered Skyler the director’s chair after the reviewers published their report.

They called it temporary authority. Six months. A defined mandate. Emergency access only. The language sounded restrained because everyone in the room knew that authority rarely remained temporary once people became afraid.

“You built the system,” the board member said.

“That is why I should not lead it.”

“You understand the risks better than anyone.”

“That is not the same as understanding people.”

The board member frowned. “You are being modest.”

“No. I am being specific.”

He had learned that institutions praised modesty when they wanted a person to accept power without appearing to seek it.

The offer included a salary, security, and a private office. It included a clause allowing him to suspend access during a verified threat. The clause was reasonable. That was what frightened him.

Reasonable authority became permanent through emergencies.

He took the contract home.

The girls were playing cards at the kitchen table. Hanna was reading a book with one hand while sorting laundry with the other.

“What is that?” she asked.

“An offer.”

“Do you want it?”

“I want what it would let me do.”

“That is not what I asked.”

He sat down.

He imagined the people he could help. The records corrected quickly. The campaigns stopped before they spread. The Society denied the space to reorganize.

He also imagined the first night he would decide that a person represented a risk. The first suspension. The first private exception. The first time someone asked him to protect the system from the people it served.

“I want it,” he said.

Hanna nodded.

“Then why refuse?”

“Because wanting power is not proof that I can use it safely.”

Luna placed a blank card on the table.

“Maybe you can share it,” she said.

Skyler looked at the card.

The board wanted one guardian. The children had offered a different model: no one held the door alone.

He returned to the library the next morning and proposed a rotating council with public minutes, recall votes, and automatic expiration of emergency authority.

The board rejected the idea.

The volunteers accepted it.

The project split again.

This time, the split did not mean failure. It meant the system had reached a question it could not answer without choosing what it valued.

Skyler declined the director’s chair.

He accepted a six-month term as one member of the council.

Not the guardian.

One person among others.

The distinction did not make him safe.

It made him visible.
