# Long-Form Expansion Batch Three

## Chapter Nineteen — Nine and Red Meridian

The first time Skyler heard Nine without the Choir behind him, the voice sounded young.

Not young in the recording. There, the voice had been sharpened by distortion and layered over a beat that sounded like a machine learning how to breathe. In person, Nine sat in the back of a closed record store wearing a gray hoodie and a coat too large for his shoulders.

Red Meridian stood beside the locked front door.

“You brought him,” Red said.

“I brought the person you wrote about,” Lena answered.

Nine looked at Skyler. “I didn’t write about you.”

“You used details from my life.”

“I used details from the public files.”

“The song mentioned my daughter’s drawing.”

Nine’s mouth tightened.

Red laughed. “Everything is public if someone makes it public.”

The record store smelled of cardboard and dust. Hundreds of empty sleeves lined the walls. The music had been removed after the owner died, but the room still remembered listening.

Skyler placed the black coin on the counter.

Nine recognized it.

“Where did you get that?”

“Thorne.”

Red reached for it. Skyler covered the coin with his hand.

“Don’t.”

Red smiled. “You’re learning.”

“Who is the Choir?” Skyler asked.

Nine looked at Red.

Red looked at the locked door.

“The Choir is not a group,” Nine said. “It is a contract.”

“Who pays?”

“People who want a story to feel inevitable.”

Nine explained that the rap battle had begun as a dispute over attribution. Red Meridian had sold a track built from leaked material. Nine had accused him of stealing the voice of a dead artist. The argument became popular. A broker offered them money to continue.

“Then the lyrics started arriving,” Nine said. “Details. Phrases. Dates. We were told they were public. Sometimes they were. Sometimes they were not.”

“You knew?” Skyler asked.

“I suspected.”

“That is not the same as knowing.”

Nine looked down.

Red stepped forward. “He knew enough to keep recording.”

“You did too,” Nine said.

“I knew it was profitable.”

The two men had built a war out of their disagreement and then discovered the war belonged to someone else.

Lena asked, “Why meet us?”

Nine pulled a small drive from his pocket.

“There is a folder called **FOURTH DOOR**.”

Skyler felt the air tighten.

“Where?”

“Inside the audio archive.”

Red said, “Do not give them that.”

“You said the truth was a weapon.”

“I said it was a product.”

Nine handed the drive to Lena.

Red moved toward him. Skyler stepped between them.

For a moment, the room held only breath.

Then Nine said, “The track you heard was not written for you. It was written about a profile of you. There is a difference.”

Skyler took the sentence home.

A person could be turned into material without ever becoming the author of the story told about them.

## Chapter Twenty-Six — Imani Vale

Imani lived above a repair shop where the sign had lost three letters.

She answered the door carrying a saucepan. Steam rose behind her. The apartment smelled of onions and cumin.

Skyler had expected a hidden room, a bank of monitors, evidence arranged like a shrine. He had not expected a woman in wool socks asking whether he had eaten.

“You are taller than your photographs,” she said.

“You are alive.”

“Both things are usually true.”

Mara stood behind him. She and Imani looked at one another without greeting.

“You brought her,” Imani said.

“She brought herself.”

“That is what people say when they want to avoid responsibility.”

Mara looked down.

Imani stepped aside.

The apartment contained no visible technology except a radio on the windowsill. A small garden grew in jars along the kitchen counter. Four plants. Four colors of ribbon tied around their stems.

Skyler noticed.

“Don’t turn my basil into a symbol,” Imani said.

He almost smiled. “I wasn’t.”

“You were thinking about it.”

She served soup. They sat around a table covered in repair manuals.

“I designed the consent layer,” Imani said. “Skyler designed the identity layer. We argued about memory.”

“You wanted records to expire,” Skyler said.

“I wanted people to have the right to outgrow their worst decision.”

“And I thought history needed continuity.”

“History needs context. Continuity without context is a prison.”

Mara asked, “Why did you leave?”

Imani put down her spoon.

“Because the board wanted to remove the refusal logs.”

Skyler remembered the meeting. The investors had called them friction. The users who declined data sharing were labeled low-value. The system had not been stolen from them. They had allowed it to be redesigned.

“You could have fought,” Mara said.

“I did.”

“You disappeared.”

“I went somewhere my name was not useful.”

The answer hurt Mara more than an accusation.

Imani looked at Skyler. “You still want the system to prove you were right.”

“I want to stop the Society.”

“That is not what I asked.”

He looked toward the radio. Static moved through it like dry grass.

“I want the past to admit it was wrong.”

“Past systems do not admit anything. People do.”

The four plants leaned toward the window.

Imani gave him a folder. Inside were original Aegis design notes, including a section marked **FOURTH DOOR: DISPUTE AND REFUSAL**.

“It was supposed to be a place where anyone could object to the system’s interpretation,” she said. “An objection could not be deleted, only answered.”

“What happened?”

“The Society turned it into a myth. They made people believe the Fourth Door was a command channel. Everyone searched for power. No one searched for accountability.”

Skyler closed the folder.

“Will you testify?”

Imani looked at him for a long time.

“If I testify, you will have to include the part where I failed.”

“Yes.”

“Not the heroic version.”

“No.”

“Then I will testify.”

At the door, Imani handed him a jar of basil.

“What is this for?” he asked.

“Something living that does not need to mean anything.”

He carried it through the city with both hands.

## Chapter Twenty-Eight — The Arbitration Council

Thorne entered the council room without an invitation because invitations were for people who believed a room belonged to someone else.

Three members waited beneath a wall of blank screens. They used names that sounded like functions: Director, Counsel, Steward. Their actual names were irrelevant to the system because the system had learned to survive without them.

The Director wore a white shirt with no tie. “You were instructed to recruit him.”

“I attempted recruitment.”

“You created an attachment.”

“Attachment increases compliance.”

“Attachment increases unpredictability.”

Thorne placed the black coin on the table.

“The coin is not the issue.”

“The coin is the issue whenever the target believes it is.”

Counsel opened a file. “Skyler Blue Spillers has become publicly sympathetic. A damaged father. A disgraced technologist. A possible whistleblower.”

“Possible is doing a great deal of work.”

“Public narratives do not require proof.”

Thorne looked at the blank screens. He had designed the first screen. It displayed every version of a target at once. Now the screens were blank because the council preferred not to look at the machinery when deciding what it had done.

“The Fourth Door is circulating,” Steward said.

“Because you allowed it.”

“Because you failed to contain it.”

Thorne smiled. “Containment is a fantasy you sell to clients.”

The room tightened.

Director said, “You have become sentimental.”

“No. I have become accurate.”

Counsel pushed a document toward him. “There is a proposal to make him the center of a public collapse.”

“A martyr?”

“A cautionary example.”

Thorne read the plan. It used the daughters’ photographs, the old legal records, the rap-battle lyrics, and a fabricated connection to an international faction. It was elegant. It was cruel. It would work.

For the first time, he felt something like disgust.

Not because of the damage. Because the plan was unimaginative.

“You want to make him unstable,” Thorne said.

“We want to make him legible.”

“No. You want to make the public believe instability explains whatever you do next.”

The Director leaned forward. “Do not pretend you are above this.”

“I am not above it.”

“Then sign.”

Thorne looked at the document.

He thought of the diner. The eggs Skyler had not ordered. The way the man had looked at the door every time someone entered. Thorne had called that fear useful.

Now he understood that usefulness was the Society’s preferred word for harm.

He signed the first page.

Then he stopped.

“This is not a complete signature,” Counsel said.

“It is complete enough to show intent.”

The Director stood. “You are choosing the target over the system.”

“No,” Thorne said. “I am choosing the system I can still control.”

The council dismissed him.

Outside, his phone displayed 11:11.

He did not believe in signs.

He believed in timing.

That belief had built his life.

It had also made him vulnerable to anyone who knew when to place a number in front of him.
