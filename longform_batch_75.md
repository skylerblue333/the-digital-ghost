# Long-Form Expansion Batch Seventy-Five

## Chapter One Hundred Eleven — The Small Archive

The children began their own archive in a cardboard box.

They collected things adults ignored: bus tickets, broken pencils, drawings of the library doors, a list of jokes that only worked in winter, and a photograph of the sky taken from the same window every afternoon for a week.

Sarah’s daughters brought the box to Skyler after school.

“Can we put it in the federation?” one asked.

“Why?”

“So no one can erase it.”

Skyler looked at the box. The lid had been decorated with four crooked circles.

“Who gets to decide what stays?”

The girls looked at each other.

“We do.”

“Then it should not belong to the federation.”

They were offended.

He explained that preservation could become another kind of taking. If the box entered the institutional archive, adults might classify it, scan it, translate it, and eventually discuss its importance without the children who had made it.

The girls decided to build a separate archive room in a closet.

They wrote rules on colored paper. No item could be added without the maker’s permission. Anyone could remove their own item. Visitors could look but not photograph. Every year, the rules would be rewritten.

The system lasted three weeks before the girls argued over a glitter-covered drawing.

One wanted it preserved. The other wanted it destroyed because the drawing showed a private joke.

Skyler watched them negotiate.

The argument became louder.

The box fell over.

The glitter covered the floor.

For a moment, he wanted to intervene and restore order.

Then he remembered the first rule: the archive belonged to them.

They resolved the dispute by cutting the drawing in half. Each child kept one side. The remaining halves could not be understood without one another.

The solution was imperfect.

It was also theirs.

At the next federation meeting, Skyler described the children’s archive without showing any contents.

Mara asked whether he had permission to describe it.

He had not.

He corrected the record.

The meeting continued without the story.

That evening, the girls added a new rule to their box:

**Adults must not use a story to prove they are good adults.**

Skyler read the sentence twice.

“Who wrote that?” he asked.

“Both of us.”

He nodded.

The rule remained.
