# Long-Form Expansion Batch Fifty-Six

## Chapter Ninety-Two — One Person Among Others

Skyler’s first day as an ordinary participant began with a rejected request.

He submitted a form asking to review an old access decision connected to the Mirror Room. The intake system assigned it a number and placed it in the queue.

No one recognized his name.

That was not entirely true. The volunteer at the desk recognized him after ten minutes and quietly offered to move the request forward.

Skyler refused.

“I will wait.”

“You helped build the system.”

“That is why I should follow it.”

The volunteer looked uncertain.

“Is this a test?”

“No.”

“Then what is it?”

“A request.”

He sat in the waiting area with six other people. One read a newspaper. One filled out a form. One stared at the door. A child played with a green card from the community deck.

Skyler waited forty minutes.

The review coordinator called his number.

She was young, tired, and unaware that the person before her had once signed the architecture documents.

“Can you explain what you are asking?” she said.

He did.

“What records do you have?”

He listed them.

“Which parts are private?”

He paused.

“Some of the file includes information about other people.”

“Then we will need to separate your request from theirs.”

He nodded.

The process took three weeks.

The final response said the access decision had been made under a policy that no longer existed. The record could not be deleted, but the refusal log would be amended to show that the policy had been withdrawn.

Skyler read the response twice.

He wanted a larger correction. A public acknowledgment. A sentence that said the system had failed him.

The response did not contain one.

It contained what could be supported.

He accepted it.

At home, Hanna asked whether the process had felt different from the other side.

“Yes.”

“How?”

“I had to trust people who did not know what I wanted the answer to be.”

She smiled.

“That sounds difficult.”

“It was.”

“Did you like it?”

Skyler thought about the waiting room, the number, the coordinator’s careful questions.

“Yes,” he said. “A little.”

The answer surprised him.

The work was safer when no one person could bend it around his own pain.
